## TL;DR

> **TL;DR:** Rise Reforming converts biogas from farms, landfills and wastewater plants into chemicals like dimethyl ether, methanol and dimethyl carbonate, using reformers that fit inside a shipping container and run on site. The Chicago startup joined Y Combinator's S26 batch and has a pilot unit due to start up at a Chicagoland wastewater plant in Q1 2027.

## What it is

Rise Reforming builds modules that turn waste gas into marketable chemicals at the site where the gas is produced. The company is based in Chicago and joined Y Combinator's S26 batch in September 2026, announcing itself on Hacker News with a Launch HN post in July.

The input is biogas, the methane-rich gas that anaerobic digesters at dairy farms, wastewater plants and food and beverage plants produce from manure, biosolids and food waste. Landfills generate it too, and it needs capture systems to be collected. Most of that gas has no buyer. The company estimates the US produces enough biogas to make over $20 billion worth of chemicals a year, and that 60% of it is wasted or flared in low-margin applications.

The pitch is to sit between the two sides. They pay a biogas producer for the gas and sell the chemicals onward, so the module removes both the cost of moving a low-value gas and the risk of long supply chains. Their first target chemical is dimethyl ether, picked because of its high-margin use in cosmetics, with methanol as the longer-term goal.

## The three-step process

Their technology page describes a patent-pending process that folds three commercialized steps into one system:

```text
raw biogas
  (landfills, farms, wastewater plants)
        |
        v
  [1] biogas cleaning    contaminants removed, composition tuned
        |
        v
  [2] bi-reforming       CH4 + CO2 + H2O + steam  ->  syngas
        |
        v
  [3] syngas upgrading   syngas  ->  DME | methanol | DMC
```

Cleaning is not trivial. Biogas carries hydrogen sulfide, and sulfur poisons the reforming catalyst, so the gas has to be scrubbed before it goes anywhere near the reactor. Rise Reforming does that work in house with a specialized gas cleaning partner, and monitors H2S continuously so a shift in gas quality gets caught before it reaches the catalyst. The founders say they have a good sense of how clean the gas can get with this system, down to low parts-per-billion levels.

In step two, treated biogas is mixed with steam and sent through the reformer, where methane, carbon dioxide and water are converted into syngas. Step three upgrades that syngas into a finished chemical. Syngas is the building block for dozens of chemicals and fuels, which is why the company claims it can serve a range of end markets from the same front end.

## What they sell

Three products, each with end uses the company has named:

| Product | End uses |
| --- | --- |
| Dimethyl ether (DME) | Aerosol propellant, propane additive, refrigerant |
| Dimethyl carbonate (DMC) | Battery production, solvent, chemical feedstock |
| Methanol | Marine fuel, solvent, chemical feedstock |

The commercial argument is price, not just carbon. They say their process produces DME that competes with petrochemical DME, and green methanol and DMC at the low end of the market. On the carbon side, biogas feedstock gives them what they describe as a 90% lower carbon footprint, and the company claims to be the first in the Americas to produce syngas from wastewater biogas.

## Sizing a candidate site

The most concrete number the founders gave came in the Hacker News thread: a site needs to produce roughly 45 SCFM of biogas, about 14,000 MMBtu a year, before installing a unit makes sense. Any wastewater plant treating more than 5 MGD will likely clear that bar.

```bash
# Site screening, using the figures from the Launch HN thread (July 2026)
min_scfm=45          # minimum biogas flow for one module
annual_mmbtu=14000  # yearly energy content at that flow

echo "one module needs ${min_scfm} SCFM of biogas (about ${annual_mmbtu} MMBtu/yr)"
echo "screening rule of thumb: wastewater plants above ~5 MGD"
```

The same thread covered the unglamorous parts. Power draw is meaningful, since reforming is endothermic, but the founders say their ASPEN simulations show utilities can host the modules without upgrades, and they plan to run at steady state rather than ramping around electricity prices. That choice pushes them toward states with cheap industrial power. On labor, early units will have an operator on site while the team learns, with the aim of remote monitoring from a central location as the fleet grows.

## What to watch for

The proof of concept ran for more than 1,800 hours, but it ran on synthetic biogas. Live biogas is messier, and the whole pilot plan depends on the 2027 unit handling it without the catalyst taking damage.

There is no commercial unit yet and no published pricing. The site has no pricing page, and the founders describe signing letters of intent and MOUs before formal agreements as they scale. A two-sided market like this means gas supply and chemical offtake have to line up at the same time, and they admit the selling side is harder than the buying side.

The community pushed back on the technical and commercial choices, and the exchange is worth reading. One commenter argued that biogas first adds risk, and that flared natural gas would be easier to scale into, since you can flare gas almost anywhere. Another, with a background in the same field, argued for building from deep principles rather than something easier first. The founders replied that flared natural gas sites are not ruled out, and that the obstacle is often logistics around where the CO2 comes from. Another commenter raised electrical interconnection as the quiet bottleneck at small sites, which the founders answered with their utility capacity claims.

The chemistry is genuinely hard. Catalyst work on methane and syngas is where most of the last 20 years of similar attempts runs into reliability problems, and that is the part that has to survive commercial operation. Methanol price volatility cuts both ways, since the founders point to a spike in global methanol prices that they attribute to the Iran war, even though the US is normally a net exporter.
