> **TL;DR:** Risklytics is a **commercial insurance brokerage** for companies whose products leave the lab, from warehouse robots to autonomous vehicles, drones and spacecraft. You describe your operation in plain English, a licensed producer reads the forms before anything binds, and the carrier pays the broker's commission out of the premium, so nothing gets stacked on top of your rate. It is a YC S26 company out of San Francisco with a real license, NPN 22311838.

## What it is

Risklytics, Inc. does business as Risklytics Insurance Services. It is a licensed insurance producer, resident in Massachusetts and licensed in nine more states, and it places commercial insurance for companies that put machines into the physical world.

The seven segments it names tell you who the customer is:

- Robotics and physical AI: machines working customer sites
- AI software and agents: models doing customer work
- Hardware and deep tech: machines built on a bench and sold to customers
- Drones and UAS: aircraft, and the companies that build and fly them
- Autonomy and autonomous vehicles: vehicles and systems that drive themselves
- Space: launch, satellites and in-orbit hardware
- Defense, DoD and SBIR contracts: insurance a government contract asks for

Ten coverage lines sit underneath that, from general liability and tech E&O through cyber, equipment, intellectual property and space.

Sam Gold and Alex Risio founded it. The company is in Y Combinator's Summer 2026 batch, based in San Francisco, and it launched on Hacker News on 26 August 2026 with 54 points and 9 comments.

## Why it matters

Commercial forms were not written for autonomy. A robot arm on a customer floor gets priced the way a forklift gets priced, and a drone gets priced the way a model aircraft gets priced. The underwriter needs to know what the machine does, where it operates, who stands next to it when it goes wrong, and what the software does when it goes wrong in a way nobody has seen before.

The submission is the product. Risklytics says the carrier sets the premium, so what the broker controls is what the underwriter sees. They submit to multiple specialist carriers, and a licensed producer reads the forms before binding.

They also run a live AI exclusions tracker, refreshed weekly, plus a guide on what to do when a renewal adds an AI exclusion. That is a real problem being sold against, not a slogan.

## Getting it running

There is no API and nothing to install. The application lives at app.risklytics.ai, and the site publishes a plain text guide that is the fastest way to get the full picture.

```bash
# the whole site as plain text
curl -s https://www.risklytics.ai/llms.txt

# where you actually apply
curl -sI https://app.risklytics.ai/sign-up/ | head -1
```

If typing is not your thing, there is a talk it through path: book a 30-minute call, an insurance expert works through the questions with you on the phone, and you end the call with the application done.

What you write into that application is the part worth preparing. The site asks for what you build and where it operates, in plain English. Here is the shape of a good answer, written for a fictional warehouse robotics company:

```text
We build autonomous warehouse robots. Units run inside customer
distribution centers in the US and Canada, mostly indoors on flat
concrete, sometimes sharing floor space with forklift traffic from
staff. Each unit weighs roughly 300 kg and travels under 2 m/s, with
a lidar obstacle avoidance stack we update remotely.

Revenue comes from robot-as-a-service contracts with three named
customers. 40 units deployed, 12 in pilots. 18 engineers on the team,
none working around heavy machinery today.

We need additional insured status on customer certificates of
insurance. No vehicles. No international operations.
```

That answers what underwriters actually ask: what the machine is, what the environment looks like, what happens when it fails, and what the contract terms are.

## What it costs

No published rates, which is normal for commercial lines and unhelpful if you wanted a number on the page. The fee structure is the interesting part, and Risklytics publishes the comparison itself.

| | Buying direct | Generic broker | Risklytics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost to apply | Free | Free | Free |
| Broker fee on top of premium | N/A | None | None |
| Application built for machines in the field | No | No | Yes |
| Submitted to multiple specialist carriers | No | No | Yes |
| A licensed producer reads the forms before binding | No | Yes | Yes |
| You can ask what we earn on your policy | N/A | No | Yes |

The commission comes out of a premium the carrier already built in, and that same commission exists whether or not a broker places the policy. A broker taking it out of the premium rather than charging you on top is standard economics. What Risklytics is selling is the submission, not a cheaper rate.

They also say plainly that you can ask what they earn on your placement and they will tell you. Worth doing before you sign anything.

## What to watch for

Coverage is still underwritten by third-party carriers, so nothing on the site is a quote or an offer, and every placement is subject to underwriting, policy terms and availability. Your timeline depends on carrier response, not on how fast you submit.

The licensing footprint is narrow. As of 10 September 2026 the recorded state licenses are:

```text
NPN 22311838
Resident: Massachusetts (3004393145)
Arizona (3004416199)       Colorado (965790)
Illinois (3004416248)       Indiana (4258322)
New Jersey (3004416802)     Ohio (1770964)
Texas (3550054)             Washington (1378046)
```

If your operations or your largest customer sit in a state that is not on that list, ask before you rely on it. The site says states get added as clients need them.

AI exclusions deserve the closest read. Most commercial liability forms predate the capability that now causes the loss, and an exclusion can sit in the liability section rather than the technology section. The guide on what to do when your renewal adds an AI exclusion, and the weekly tracker, are the most practically useful pages on the whole domain if you are signing an E&O or cyber policy.

One structural note. Risklytics runs two businesses: it places coverage for startups, and it has a page aimed at insurers who want to write frontier tech risk. If you are a carrier, the interesting surface is different, but the licensing and the editorial policy still apply.

The guide on why robotics applications get declined is worth reading before you apply. It describes where each segment trips the wire, and the fix is usually more documentation rather than a different product.
