> **TL;DR:** Docket stores meeting notes as timestamped entries rather than one long document, so every attendee gets their own section and actions can be tracked across meetings. The cloud version is flat priced with a free tier, and a self-hosted edition runs on your own server through a single Docker install command. It is proprietary software, and the paid self-hosted tier is a $995 per year license.

## What it is

Docket is a web app for people who sit in a lot of recurring meetings and want notes that stay useful weeks later. The developer built it after running roughly a dozen one-on-ones a week and losing track of what had been agreed. The premise is narrow on purpose: type with one hand during a meeting, keep the notes short, and track who owes what.

The unit of content is an entry. A doc is a stack of entries, each timestamped, and a new entry is one click away with a template applied. That matters more than it sounds, because it is the opposite of how a general document editor works. You never scroll through last month's meeting to find what was decided, and you never fight a shared Google Doc where four people have rearranged the same headings.

Sharing is also opinionated. When a doc is shared, each collaborator gets their own section instead of everyone editing the same block of text. The result is that you can always see who wrote what, and each person keeps a private running log of their own notes.

## The pieces that carry the load

Actions are the part worth stealing. They are checkboxes with mentions attached, and they live in one place across every workspace rather than being buried in whichever meeting they came up in. You can filter to see whether an action from three weeks ago is still open. On the Pro plan, admins get an Audit View that reaches archived and deleted entries, which is the sort of feature that shows up in a compliance review rather than a launch post.

The rest is deliberately plain: bullet-friendly formatting, pasted images and links, and a search that reaches across every doc you have. The developer was explicit that he did not want a tool where you accidentally create a spreadsheet or order pizza for the office while your attention is on the people in the room. That stance shows up in the HN discussion too, where the most common reaction was that the product feels focused rather than general purpose.

## Pricing

The cloud product avoids per-seat pricing, which is unusual for a team tool and the main reason the author says people should try it.

| Plan | Price | Workspaces | Docs per workspace | Export |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Free | $0 | Up to 5 | Up to 5 | Text |
| Plus | $49 per month | Unlimited | Unlimited | Text, PDF with images |
| Pro | $99 per month | Unlimited | Unlimited | Text, PDF with images |

Plus adds dedicated support, Pro adds priority support and the audit and compliance tools. Paying annually drops the effective price to $10 per month on Plus and $20 per month on Pro. There is also a launch special plan at $1 per month with unlimited workspaces and docs for people who signed up early, grandfathered in for whatever plan comes after it.

## Running it on your own server

The self-hosted edition came about because people in the launch thread said they could not put confidential meeting notes through a hosted service without a security review they did not have time to run. That feedback shipped a few months later as a Docker-based build that needs a Linux, macOS, or Windows machine with Docker installed.

You need a server, and you need to decide on a domain before you start. After signing in to your Docket account, the site hands you a personal install script:

```bash
source <(curl -fsSL https://withdocket.com/install/YOUR-TOKEN)
```

The installer sets up the container, and if you point a DNS A record at the server it will provision a Let's Encrypt certificate so the app is served over HTTPS on your domain. Without a custom domain it runs on `localhost` on a port you choose. For anything beyond a private network, put a reverse proxy in front of it, and the docs suggest running Caddy or Nginx in a container since Docker is already there.

Self-hosted has its own two-tier pricing:

| Plan | Price | Seats | Extras |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Free | $0 | Up to 3 | Core features |
| Self-Hosted Pro | $995 per year | Unlimited | Priority support |

The paid tier is a one-year license that renews annually. You are granted a new key on renewal, and cancelling stops the renewal while letting the current license run to its expiry date.

## What to watch for

Docket is not open source. The company publishes a "Docket Software License Agreement" covering the core container image, the CLI, the installer, and deployment tooling, with the document versioned separately from the software itself. The self-hosted edition gives you control of the data and the deployment, but the software itself stays closed. A third-party licenses page lists the open-source components inside the build, mostly MIT.

The pricing for a single-person self-hosted deployment is worth thinking about carefully. $995 a year for one seat is a lot next to a cloud plan that starts at $49 a month for a whole team, and the free tier covers three seats. If nobody on your team wants to self-host, cloud is simply cheaper. Self-hosting makes sense when a compliance requirement forces it, or when you want the notes to never leave infrastructure you control.

One more quirk: workspace creation is rate limited on every plan, including paid ones, so a large migration may need to be paced. The developer frames this as protecting service quality, but it is the kind of thing that bites during onboarding.

The developer also writes publicly about the project, including a post explaining why he is building a note-taking app without AI. If you want to understand the design decisions rather than just the feature list, that is worth reading.
