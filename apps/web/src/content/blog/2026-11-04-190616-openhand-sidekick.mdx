> **TL;DR:** Sidekick is a desktop app that joins your telehealth video call, transcribes it live, and hands back a recap with questions you might not have thought to ask. OpenHand says it never keeps visit audio, though the privacy policy names transcription and AI vendors in the processing chain, so read that part before you point it at a doctor.

## What it is

OpenHand is a healthcare company, and Sidekick is the piece they opened up first. It is a desktop app for macOS and Windows that you start before a telehealth appointment. While the appointment runs, it does four things:

- Transcribes what is said, in real time
- Defines medical terms as they come up
- Suggests questions worth asking while there is still time to ask them
- Assembles your symptoms, questions and medications into a summary

When the visit ends you get the recap, the definitions it surfaced and your next steps in one place.

The constraint that shapes everything is where it runs. Sidekick does not join the call as a participant and it does not sit in a browser tab. It lives on the same computer you take the video visit on, listening through the system audio of whatever conferencing product you already use. So telehealth on a laptop works today. Telehealth from your phone does not.

OpenHand is also careful about what this is not. No diagnosis, no treatment, no prescriptions. In their own framing, a care preparation and cost-navigation co-pilot. The bill review feature that was OpenHand's original product is coming back in the mobile app later.

## Getting it running

Early access needs an invite code. The request form is a short Google Form, and codes arrive by email with the subject "OpenHand Sidekick - Early Access Invite". Worth searching your inbox for that subject if you already signed up, since invites go out as places open.

Before installing, confirm your machine clears the requirement. Sidekick ships as a universal macOS binary that needs macOS 13 or later:

```bash
sw_vers -productVersion
```

If that prints 13 or higher you are fine. On Windows the build ships through the Microsoft Store, and `winver` is the equivalent check.

Then pull it down. macOS gets a direct disk image:

```bash
# Drag OpenHand into Applications from the mounted image
open https://stdist00prod00openhand.blob.core.windows.net/releases/OpenHand.dmg
```

There is no Homebrew cask for it, which matters if you install apps through casks. Windows users take the Microsoft Store route rather than a direct download.

Either way you sign in with the invite code, then press Start Visit before the appointment. Sidekick only captures while it is switched on, which means the button is a real consent decision rather than a formality.

## What happens to your visit data

This is the section worth reading, because the marketing page and the privacy policy are not the same sentence.

The homepage says HIPAA-aligned by design, no ads, and no selling of data. The privacy policy, last updated September 10, 2026, is more specific. Audio from your visit is processed in real time to produce transcripts, notes, summaries and next steps. OpenHand states it does not store or retain visit audio recordings. It also lists service providers covering hosting, storage, transcription and AI processing, who may process information under OpenHand's agreements.

So the accurate reading is that OpenHand is not keeping the recording, not that the audio stays on your machine. The transcription and summarization work happens somewhere.

The same policy is direct about another gap. HIPAA does not necessarily apply to information you hand to a consumer health app directly, as opposed to Sidekick being supplied to you by a covered healthcare organization under a business associate agreement. If you get Sidekick through a provider, that agreement governs instead.

On model training, the policy says no training on identifiable health data. Usage analysis to improve the product uses de-identified and aggregated data, and you can opt out of even that.

## What to watch for

Recording a conversation carries its own rules, and the privacy policy puts that responsibility on you rather than the software. OpenHand's FAQ notes that twelve US states require everyone in the conversation to agree before you can record. Their suggestion is to say it out loud at the start of the visit:

```text
"Is it okay if I use my healthcare notetaker? It helps me understand and keep track of my care."
```

Clinicians are generally fine with it once they know what it is for. If the clinician you are with would rather you did not, turn it off.

Early access is free, but the waitlist is prioritized, so there is no guarantee on timing. The privacy policy also says data is retained for as long as reasonably necessary to provide and secure the service, with limited copies potentially remaining in backups and service-provider systems until their normal retention periods expire. That is a longer and vaguer horizon than the one-line claim on the homepage.

Then there is what a summary does when the visit was complicated. A recap that flattens a multi-condition conversation into bullet points is going to lose things, and the product gives you no signal about which parts it was unsure of.
