> **TL;DR:** **Bloffy** is a hosted blogging platform built by one developer in Korea for people who want a fast blog on their own domain without running a server. A single post can hold Markdown, raw HTML, CSS and JavaScript, Mermaid diagrams render natively, and the Terms of Service say the service is free of charge during beta.

## What Bloffy is

Bloffy started as a personal project. Its maker, who posts as textlanes33 on Hacker News, spent roughly two years rebuilding his own blog after taking it off Next.js and ended up with a 99 to 100 PageSpeed score. Bloffy is that setup turned into a hosted product.

The editor is a split view with source on the left and a live preview on the right. What makes it different is what a post is allowed to contain: Markdown, raw HTML, CSS and JavaScript all live in the same document. Block editors impose their own document model, so anything they cannot represent turns into a workaround, and you end up fighting the toolbar instead of writing. Bloffy treats the post as source code.

Around that editor sit the usual blog parts. Drafts autosave, each post carries a byline and a reading time estimate, and you set the thumbnail, meta description and tags per post. A translation workflow can fork a post into another language on its own URL, and several members can publish to the same blog with different roles.

## Why raw HTML still matters

Blogging platforms tend to be fine right up until you want something slightly off-template. Then you are fighting a page builder, or you drop down to a static site generator and take on a build pipeline, a deploy hook and a hosting bill.

Bloffy sits in the gap between a hosted platform and a site you run yourself. You get a real blog on your own domain, served for you, and the markup stays yours.

The maker's write-up on removing Next.js from his blog hit 198 points on Hacker News, and the Show HN for the platform itself reached 30. The comment threads are worth reading because that is where the tradeoffs get stated out loud. One commenter pointed out that letting bloggers run their own ads would wreck their scores, and the maker agreed, noting that his current numbers are lab data rather than field measurements.

## Getting it running

Sign up, create a blog, pick a theme, publish. Bloffy serves blogs on a bloffy.blog address out of the box, and you can connect a domain you own from the blog settings once the site is live.

A post is a text file with a title, and the Markdown version of one looks like this:

```markdown
## The Bloffy editor is genuinely convenient

Editor on the left, preview on the right: a split-view writing experience.

<style>
.highlight {
  background-image: linear-gradient(90deg, rgba(91, 46, 229, 0.7), rgba(139, 107, 255, 0.4));
  color: white;
}
</style>
```

Notice the style block sitting in the middle of the prose. That is allowed, and it is the whole difference between Bloffy and a block editor. Mermaid diagrams work the same way, fenced inside the post and themed with the blog's own color tokens:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  Q1([I want a blog of my own.]) -->|Yes| Q2([Self-hosting is too much upkeep.])
  Q2 -->|Yes| Q3([Performance matters to me.])
  Q3 -->|Yes| B[Bloffy]
```

If the default look is not what you want, a theme is just a set of CSS variables you can override in blog settings:

```css
:root {
  --color-surface-1: #ededea;
  --color-surface-2: #e4e4e0;
  --color-hairline: #dcdcd7;
}
```

Overriding one of them does not touch the original, so later revisions of the theme will not reach your copy. There is also a gallery of 18 themes, from magazine covers to literary quarterlies to photo mosaics, plus a separate gallery of reusable HTML snippets you can drop into posts.

## Where the speed comes from

| What Bloffy does | Why it matters for the score |
| --- | --- |
| Uploads get a srcset with four widths | Each device downloads an image close to its own size |
| The CDN recompresses for the browser it sees | WebP or AVIF wherever the browser supports it |
| Original width and height are kept on file | The markup can reserve space, so images do not shift the page |
| Title, summary and image are pushed out on publish | Feeds and social previews fill in without manual work |
| No client framework ships with the page | Little JavaScript to parse before the first render |

The result on the sample blog is a 99 to 100 PageSpeed score across Web Vitals, performance, best practices, SEO and accessibility. The maker is careful to add that this varies a little from blog to blog, and that he wants to re-measure once there is real traffic rather than lab data.

## What to watch for

The biggest practical surprise is search visibility. A new blog starts as "not reviewed" and is served with a noindex tag, so it will not show up in search results at all. Once you have published posts, the blog enters a review queue automatically, there is nothing to apply for, and a person approves it. Approval lifts the noindex tag and pings IndexNow, but it can still take a while to appear. Plan for a blog that is invisible to Google on day one.

Translations are manual. The Create translation button copies a post into another language with a new URL, and it does not translate the text for you. The maker says he is considering automatic translation and still recommends doing it by hand, because machine translation tends to flatten what you meant.

Pricing is not published. The Terms of Service say the service is currently provided free of charge, that the beta tier applies to all workspaces, and that when the beta ends or paid tiers appear, the fees, payment and refund terms are set separately and communicated to members at least 30 days before they take effect. Free now, with a priced future that nobody has decided yet.

It is a hosted service rather than open source, and it is run by a single developer in Korea with no company behind it. The Terms of Service describe a fairly strict operator model where a blog can be hidden, a post unpublished or an account suspended, with email as the only appeal channel. That is a lot of control to hand to one person, and worth weighing if the blog matters to you.

If you plan to monetize with ads, expect the performance numbers to fall. Nothing in Bloffy stops you running them, but a script-heavy ad slot works directly against the scores that make the platform attractive in the first place.

## Who it fits

The landing page puts it as three questions: do you want a blog of your own, is self-hosting too much upkeep, does performance matter to you. If all three are yes, Bloffy is worth a try.

If you only need somewhere to write and do not care about the domain or the markup, a platform blog is fine and you will save yourself the setup. If you want total control and are willing to run the stack, self-hosting still gives you more. Bloffy is aimed at the person in between, who wants a real site without maintaining a server.
